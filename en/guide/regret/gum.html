014 real reasons people regret Gum (Periodontal) Treatment

Periodontitis is one of the most common chronic diseases among Korean adults. Yet the reason regrets about Gum (Periodontal) Treatment are so great is that it involves irreversible loss (bone).

Regret 1"Why didn’t I do this sooner?" — the regret of waiting too long
Why it happensGum disease progresses with little or no pain. Even if you see bleeding, you might think, "I brushed too hard," and by the time your teeth feel loose, more than half the supporting bone is often already gone. Lost bone does not regenerate naturally — that is the essence of gum-disease regret.
PreventionGum bleeding isn’t normal — it’s a sign of inflammationIf you have bleeding, bad breath, or a spongy/loose feeling, get a gum exam (periodontal pocket charting) right away. The sooner you come, the simpler the treatment and the better your chances of keeping your teeth.
Regret 2"My teeth got more sensitive after treatment"
Why it happensWhen tartar under the gums is removed and swollen gums settle down, previously covered tooth roots can become exposed, causing sensitivity. Without understanding this, some think "treatment damaged my gums" and stop care prematurely.
PreventionSensitivity is part of your gums returning to a healthy positionMost people adapt within a few weeks, and it’s managed with desensitizing toothpaste and fluoride application. When this change is explained beforehand, it won’t be alarming.
Regret 3"Gaps opened between my teeth, and they look longer"
Why it happensAs swollen gums recede, spaces between teeth become visible and teeth look longer. It’s a cosmetic concern, but it reflects swollen, abnormal tissue returning to normal.
PreventionThe exposed space is a space to clean with an interdental brushIf front-tooth aesthetics are a major concern, you can discuss add-on options after treatment, such as prosthetics or gum contouring.
Regret 4Relapse — "I skipped maintenance visits"
Why it happensGum (Periodontal) Treatment manages a condition, much like hypertension or diabetes. If you skip 3~6 months maintenance (regular Scaling (Dental Cleaning) and checkups) after finishing treatment, bacteria build up again and periodontitis quietly returns. The second round of treatment is harder than the first.
PreventionThe end of Gum (Periodontal) Treatment is the start of maintenanceBooking your 3~6 months maintenance schedule right when treatment ends is the most reliable way to prevent relapse.

02Gum (Periodontal) Treatment side effects — frequency and what to do

Most symptoms that seem like "side effects" of Gum (Periodontal) Treatment are actually signs your gums are returning to normal. The real risk lies not in treatment, but in leaving the disease untreated.

Side effectFrequency/PatternWhat to do
Sensitivity after treatmentCommon — exposed root areas; adapt within a few weeksDesensitizing toothpaste and fluoride application. If it persists, come in.
Spaces between teeth; teeth look longerCommon — a normal change as swelling subsidesStart interdental brush care. Cosmetic refinements require a separate consultation
Pain or throbbing during/after treatmentOccasionally — for a few days after deep periodontal treatmentTake prescribed medication, avoid irritating foods
Temporary increase in tooth mobilityOccasionally — when tartar had been acting like a splintStabilizes as gums heal. If severe, consider tooth splinting
Gum recession may progressAfter treatment of severe periodontitisIt’s a result of the disease, not a side effect of treatment. The goal is to prevent further recession
RecurrenceCommon if maintenance is stoppedMaintenance every 3~6 months is the only prevention
These are general trends and may vary with your oral condition and overall health. For an accurate risk assessment, please consult with your clinician.

03Please think again if any of these apply to you

To be frank, Gum (Periodontal) Treatment isn’t the right answer for everyone. If any of the following apply, discuss alternatives with your dentist before rushing into treatment.

  • Very few people are "not suited" for Gum (Periodontal) Treatment — however, a tooth with extreme bone loss that cannot be savedmay be better managed with extraction followed by a restoration plan than by trying to force it to stay. Ask for a clear explanation of which teeth can be saved and which should be removed.
  • Uncontrolled diabetes — gum healing is slow and relapse is fast. Coordinated medical care is needed for good results (gum disease and diabetes worsen each other in a two-way relationship).
  • Currently smoking — smoking greatly reduces the effectiveness of Gum (Periodontal) Treatment. Use this treatment as a chance to make a quit plan.
  • Not planning to attend maintenance — if you only get treatment and skip maintenance, recurrence is just a matter of time. Commit to a maintenance schedule before starting.

04Pre-treatment checklist — Confirm these and you’ll greatly reduce your chances of regret

Whichever dental clinic you choose is fine. If the clinic can answer the points below clearly, you can feel confident receiving care there.

  • Gum exam (measuring pocket depths) and X-rays to assess bone statuswere done
  • Heard an explanation of which teeth can be saved and which are difficult
  • Was informed about the treatment steps (Scaling (Dental Cleaning) → root planing → surgery if needed) and the costs
  • Post-treatment sensitivity and spacing changes are normalI understood this
  • 3~6 months maintenance schedulewas scheduled
  • Made a plan to manage risk factors such as diabetes and smoking

05Frequently Asked Questions

Q.Will my gums grow back after Gum (Periodontal) Treatment?
We can control inflammation with treatment, but jawbone supporting the teeth that’s already been lost to periodontitis does not naturally regenerate. That’s why early treatment is everything. In select cases, we can attempt partial recovery with Bone Graft (GBR) and regenerative procedures, but candidacy is limited.
Q.After Gum (Periodontal) Treatment, my teeth feel sensitive and look more spaced. Was something done wrong?
No. As swollen gums heal and shrink, root surfaces and spaces that were previously hidden become visible. Sensitivity usually settles within a few weeks, and the spaces can be managed with an interdental brush. The swollen state was abnormal; what you see now is normal.
Q.Can I just take medication for my gums?
Medication is only an adjunct; the tartar and bacterial biofilm that cause gum disease must be physically removed. If you rely on medicine alone, bone destruction will continue. If you have bleeding or bad breath, a gum evaluation should come before taking medication.
Q.How many visits do I need for Gum (Periodontal) Treatment?
It depends on severity. If it’s at the gingivitis level, 1~2 sessions of Scaling (Dental Cleaning) are usually enough. For periodontitis, we divide the mouth into sections and perform multiple sessions of scaling and root planing; deeper areas may require periodontal surgery. After treatment, maintenance continues every 3~6 months.
Q.If my gums aren’t healthy, can I still get a Dental Implant?
Placing a Dental Implant while periodontitis is uncontrolled carries a high risk of peri-implantitis. The order is always gum treatment first, implant later. Your ability to maintain your gums ultimately determines implant longevity.
