Before choosing treatment, start with what worries you

Cheonan dental implant revisionAfter one difficult treatment, how do you trust the next decision?

A loose or painful implant can bring disappointment before anything else. You may worry that the whole implant must come out, that another operation will fail, or that you will be blamed for not cleaning well enough. The first useful question is where the problem lies, not which new brand to buy.

01 / The questions behind the treatment name

You deserve to understand what this means for your life.

01

Does looseness mean the whole implant failed?

Not necessarily. A loose restoration and a loose implant body require different assessments.

02

Can an inflamed implant be kept?

Sometimes, depending on stability, tissue damage, cleaning access and the response to treatment.

03

Can it be removed and replaced immediately?

That depends on remaining bone, infection and achievable stability. A staged alternative may be safer.

02 / Benefits and burdens

Benefits and burdens

What may improve

Identifying the problem precisely may allow sound components to be retained.

What to weigh up

Removal, grafting and a new restoration can require additional visits and tissue healing.

The question that helps you choose

Understand the evidence for preservation or removal and how the next plan addresses the original difficulty.

03 / Make sense of your own situation

What changes the choice of treatment?

01

Separate the tooth, connecting parts and implant body

A loose crown or screw is different from movement of the implant body in bone. Examination and imaging help identify inflammation, bone loss, component damage and bite-related issues. Previous implant cards and radiographs are helpful, but missing records are not a reason to give up seeking an assessment.

02

Compare keeping the implant with removing it

Some problems can be addressed through cleaning, component or restoration repair, or treatment around a stable implant. Other findings may make removal necessary. Ask what evidence supports the recommendation, how a preservation attempt will be reviewed and which findings would change the plan. Neither preservation nor replacement guarantees success.

03

Rebuild a manageable plan

Removal may leave reduced bone or require staged healing and grafting. Immediate replacement cannot be assumed. Discuss temporary teeth, eating and visits during the interval. The new plan should also address why cleaning, food trapping or component problems were difficult before; replacing the fixture alone may leave those problems unchanged.

Benefits and burdens

Potential benefits

Repair may be possible without replacing the entire implant system.

Reassessment can improve cleaning access and clarify a realistic maintenance plan.

Benefits and burdens

Limitations and risks

Previous tissue loss or unavailable components can make treatment more complicated.

Inflammation or mechanical problems may recur even after treatment.

Reassessing and revising a failed implant site

Treatment of an implant problem depends on its cause. The pathway below applies when the existing implant cannot be retained: after removal and site care, recovery is assessed to decide whether and when replacement is suitable.

  1. 01

    Assess the cause and retention

    Inflammation, stability and prosthetic problems are distinguished before deciding whether removal is needed.

  2. 02

    Remove and treat the site

    When needed, the implant is removed with attention to surrounding anatomy and the site is treated.

  3. 03

    Reassess and place a replacement

    The site is reassessed after any needed augmentation and healing. If conditions permit replacement, an implant is placed in the planned position; healing and stability are then reviewed before the new restoration.

  4. 04

    Restore and address risks

    The new restoration is checked for bite and cleaning access, with management of relevant risk factors.

04 / Treatment and everyday life

Plan for the time between appointments, too.

Keep any detached component, but do not glue it back or tighten it at home.

Avoid hard chewing on a loose restoration and contact the dental team promptly.

Costs, time and follow-up

Ask for a plan you can understand and manage.

What does the estimate include?

Ask which examinations, temporary stages, final treatment, retainers or maintenance, and repairs are included. The scope depends on your findings and chosen plan. A headline price cannot describe every person’s total cost. Ask how a change in the plan would be explained and agreed before it goes ahead.

How will visits fit into my life?

Bring previous records if you have them, your medication list and the practical limits on travel or time off work. Ask what each visit is for, what may change the timetable, and who to contact if something feels wrong. You do not need to promise to start treatment at the first conversation.

05 / Questions worth asking again

Uncertainty is a good reason to ask another question.

Is bleeding around an implant normal?

It warrants assessment of the surrounding tissues. Lack of pain does not rule out inflammation or bone loss.

Why does the screw keep coming loose?

The component, fit, bite and restoration design need assessment; repeatedly tightening it may not address the underlying problem.

Do I need the original clinic’s records?

They can help identify the system and compare changes. Ask which records would be most useful if you can obtain them.

Will another implant last longer?

No individual outcome can be promised. Ask which identified risks and practical problems the revised plan will change.

Take these questions to your consultation
  • Which part has the problem: body, screw, restoration or surrounding tissues?
  • What findings would make preservation inappropriate?
  • What will I wear and eat if removal is needed?
