Before choosing treatment, start with what worries you

Cheonan orthodontic treatmentIs the wish to straighten your teeth mixed with fear of starting?

You may notice your teeth most in photographs, yet worry more about extractions, pain or years of appointments than about the appearance of braces. Adults can also wonder whether they have waited too long. Begin with the change you want and the condition of your teeth, gums and bite—not a choice of appliance.

01 / The questions behind the treatment name

You deserve to understand what this means for your life.

01

Will I need teeth removed?

That depends on space, bite and treatment objectives; appliance choice alone does not decide it.

02

Am I too old for orthodontics?

Age alone is not the answer. Teeth, supporting tissues and health need assessment.

03

Will my teeth stay straight afterwards?

Long-term retention is important; movement can occur after active treatment.

02 / Benefits and burdens

Benefits and burdens

What may improve

Orthodontics can improve alignment and selected bite relationships.

What to weigh up

Appointments, daily care, discomfort and long-term retention require commitment.

The question that helps you choose

Match the treatment objective and tissue health to an appliance you can manage.

03 / Make sense of your own situation

What changes the choice of treatment?

01

Define the goal beyond a straight front row

Assessment considers alignment, how the teeth meet, roots and gum support. A small visible overlap can involve a wider bite issue, while some concerns may suit a limited plan. Ask which problems the proposed treatment addresses and which it would leave unchanged. Alternatives and the choice not to proceed should be explained.

02

Choose an appliance around the required movement

Fixed braces and removable aligners place different demands on daily life. Visibility, cleaning, eating, wearing consistency and unscheduled repairs matter. An appliance name does not decide whether extraction is needed or guarantee a shorter course. Tooth movement, available space and the overall objective guide those decisions.

03

Plan for the finish before the start

Teeth can move again after active treatment, so retainers and reviews belong in the original discussion. Treatment also carries risks, including decay when cleaning is inadequate and changes to roots or gums. Agree how these will be monitored, how discomfort is managed and what happens if the planned movement is not progressing as expected.

Benefits and burdens

Potential benefits

Tooth position can be changed without simply covering the visible surfaces.

A comprehensive assessment helps distinguish cosmetic concerns from bite and tissue issues.

Benefits and burdens

Limitations and risks

Root changes, gum problems, decay and relapse are among the risks to discuss.

The final result and duration cannot be guaranteed from a photograph or package name.

Treatment and everyday life

Four things to clarify together

  1. 01

    Explain the difficult moments

    Describe when you notice the problem, what you hope will change, and any previous experience you do not want to repeat.

  2. 02

    Compare realistic choices

    Ask which choices are suitable after examination, what each can and cannot change, and whether another problem needs attention first.

  3. 03

    Understand the stages

    Separate assessment, active treatment, healing or adaptation, and follow-up. Ask what would make the plan change.

  4. 04

    Plan ongoing care

    Check home care, review appointments and a route to advice. Long-term care can remain important after active treatment ends.

04 / Treatment and everyday life

Plan for the time between appointments, too.

Keep routine dental checks and clean carefully around any appliance.

Report breakages or poor fit and follow the agreed retainer plan after active treatment.

Costs, time and follow-up

Ask for a plan you can understand and manage.

What does the estimate include?

Ask which examinations, temporary stages, final treatment, retainers or maintenance, and repairs are included. The scope depends on your findings and chosen plan. A headline price cannot describe every person’s total cost. Ask how a change in the plan would be explained and agreed before it goes ahead.

How will visits fit into my life?

Bring previous records if you have them, your medication list and the practical limits on travel or time off work. Ask what each visit is for, what may change the timetable, and who to contact if something feels wrong. You do not need to promise to start treatment at the first conversation.

05 / Questions worth asking again

Uncertainty is a good reason to ask another question.

Are aligners always better than braces?

No. The required movements and your ability to follow the wearing plan affect suitability.

Can I straighten only one tooth?

Sometimes a limited plan is possible, but the whole bite and remaining compromises need assessment first.

Does discomfort mean something has gone wrong?

Some discomfort can accompany movement, but severe, persistent or unusual symptoms should be discussed rather than endured silently.

What should the estimate include?

Clarify records, active treatment, additional appliances or refinements, retainers and follow-up.

Take these questions to your consultation
  • Which problems will this plan improve and leave unchanged?
  • What are the alternatives to extraction or to this appliance?
  • How will roots, gums and retention be monitored?
