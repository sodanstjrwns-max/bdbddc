Before choosing treatment, start with what worries you

Cheonan guided implant surgeryCan a digital plan make a difficult decision easier to understand?

Seeing a three-dimensional plan may be reassuring, especially if you worry about a nearby nerve or the position of the final tooth. It can also be hard to know what “navigation” actually adds. A digital tool should help explain a plan; it should not replace a discussion of the limitations and alternatives.

Does navigation eliminate nerve risk?

No. It supports planning but does not remove anatomical or surgical risks.

Is guided surgery always flapless?

No. Guidance and the choice of incision are separate aspects of treatment.

What to weigh up

The question that helps you choose

Ask what specific problem guidance helps address in your case.

03 / Make sense of your own situation

What changes the choice of treatment?

01

Plan the tooth as well as the implant

Imaging and oral records can help plan implant position and direction in relation to the intended restoration. A guide may transfer aspects of that plan during surgery. The quality of the records, the fit of the guide and the actual tissues remain important. No system makes surgical placement entirely error-free.

02

Understand what the guide cannot decide

The guide does not remove the need to assess bone, gums, infection or medical conditions. Grafting and an incision may still be appropriate. Ask why guidance is useful in your particular site and whether a different approach would achieve the treatment objective with a different burden.

03

Keep room for clinical judgement

Conditions during surgery may not fully match the planned model. The team may need to adjust the approach or sequence. Discuss how that would be handled, together with temporary teeth and postoperative reviews. Guided placement does not automatically establish that an implant can be loaded immediately.

Benefits and burdens

Potential benefits

A visual plan can clarify the relationship between bone, implants and the planned teeth.

A surgical guide can assist transfer of an assessed position and direction.

Limitations and risks

No fixed accuracy or absence of complications can be promised for an individual operation.

Additional imaging, planning stages or guide production may add time and cost.

Treatment and everyday life

Four things to clarify together

  1. 01

    Explain the difficult moments

    Describe when you notice the problem, what you hope will change, and any previous experience you do not want to repeat.

  2. 02

    Compare realistic choices

    Ask which choices are suitable after examination, what each can and cannot change, and whether another problem needs attention first.

  3. 03

    Understand the stages

    Separate assessment, active treatment, healing or adaptation, and follow-up. Ask what would make the plan change.

  4. 04

    Plan ongoing care

    Check home care, review appointments and a route to advice. Long-term care can remain important after active treatment ends.

04 / Treatment and everyday life

Plan for the time between appointments, too.

Tell the team if a temporary restoration or oral condition changes after records are taken.

Follow the same individual healing and review instructions as for other implant procedures.

Costs, time and follow-up

Ask for a plan you can understand and manage.

What does the estimate include?

Ask which examinations, temporary stages, final treatment, retainers or maintenance, and repairs are included. The scope depends on your findings and chosen plan. A headline price cannot describe every person’s total cost. Ask how a change in the plan would be explained and agreed before it goes ahead.

How will visits fit into my life?

Bring previous records if you have them, your medication list and the practical limits on travel or time off work. Ask what each visit is for, what may change the timetable, and who to contact if something feels wrong. You do not need to promise to start treatment at the first conversation.

05 / Questions worth asking again

Uncertainty is a good reason to ask another question.

Does a guide avoid bone grafting?

No. The planned position still needs suitable supporting bone.

Is a guided implant necessarily faster to heal?

The guidance method does not remove biological healing requirements.

Can the plan change during surgery?

Yes. Actual conditions can require adaptation; discuss the possible reasons before consenting.

What should I see during consultation?

Ask to understand the proposed tooth position, anatomical constraints, alternatives and the role of the guide.

Take these questions to your consultation
  • Why is a guide useful for my particular site?
  • Which limitations remain after digital planning?
  • What would require a change during surgery?
