Before choosing treatment, start with what worries you

Cheonan tooth decay treatmentIf nothing hurts, how do you know whether a cavity needs treatment?

Being told you have several cavities can feel confusing when you have no symptoms. You may fear that a small filling will turn into root canal treatment, or feel ashamed that decay returned despite brushing. Start with the location, activity and depth of each lesion. Different findings do not all need the same response.

01 / The questions behind the treatment name

You deserve to understand what this means for your life.

01

Does a painless tooth need a filling?

Sometimes. Pain alone cannot determine lesion activity or depth.

02

Will deep decay always need root canal treatment?

No. Pulp assessment and remaining tissue guide the options and their limits.

03

Why does decay return around a filling?

The margin, contact, cleaning and ongoing decay risk need assessment together.

02 / Benefits and burdens

Benefits and burdens

What may improve

Treatment can address active decay while preserving appropriate tooth structure.

What to weigh up

Restoration may not be the only stage, and deep lesions can involve the pulp.

The question that helps you choose

Choose preventive care, monitoring or restoration according to the individual lesion.

03 / Make sense of your own situation

What changes the choice of treatment?

01

Separate early changes from a cavity that needs restoration

Not every dark mark is active decay, and not every early lesion requires drilling. Examination, appropriate imaging and risk assessment help distinguish staining, changes that can be managed preventively and damage that needs restoration. Ask what evidence supports treating or monitoring each area and how monitoring will be reviewed.

02

Preserve useful tissue while managing deeper disease

When decay is deep, pulp condition and the remaining tooth structure influence the options. Depth alone does not automatically settle the need for root canal treatment. The plan may change as the tooth is assessed during care. Ask about the aim, uncertainty and next step if the pulp cannot be preserved.

03

Look for why decay keeps returning

The edge of an old filling, food-trapping contact, dry mouth and frequent sugar exposure may all deserve attention. A new filling alone does not address every risk. Discuss practical cleaning and eating routines without treating recurrence as a personal failure. A plan should include both the damaged tooth and prevention of further lesions.

Benefits and burdens

Potential benefits

Early risk management may help some lesions avoid a larger restoration.

A suitable restoration can rebuild a damaged surface and improve cleanability.

Benefits and burdens

Limitations and risks

The true extent of damage may be clearer during treatment than on initial images.

New decay can occur around restored teeth if risk factors persist.

Treat decay according to its extent

Lesion depth and pulp condition guide decay treatment. The pathway below applies when a direct restoration is suitable, followed by care to reduce the risk of further decay.

  1. 01

    Assess depth and pulp

    Symptoms and lesion extent guide the choice between preventive care, direct restoration and other treatment.

  2. 02

    Prepare what is needed

    Preserve appropriate tooth structure while preparing the selected area for a reliable restoration and seal.

  3. 03

    Restore the defect

    Place and set the selected material to restore tooth form and contact.

  4. 04

    Check and prevent recurrence

    Review margins and bite, then address cleaning, diet and follow-up.

04 / Treatment and everyday life

Plan for the time between appointments, too.

Use the individual fluoride and cleaning advice provided for your risk.

Report prolonged sensitivity, biting pain or food trapping after restoration.

Costs, time and follow-up

Ask for a plan you can understand and manage.

What does the estimate include?

Ask which examinations, temporary stages, final treatment, retainers or maintenance, and repairs are included. The scope depends on your findings and chosen plan. A headline price cannot describe every person’s total cost. Ask how a change in the plan would be explained and agreed before it goes ahead.

How will visits fit into my life?

Bring previous records if you have them, your medication list and the practical limits on travel or time off work. Ask what each visit is for, what may change the timetable, and who to contact if something feels wrong. You do not need to promise to start treatment at the first conversation.

05 / Questions worth asking again

Uncertainty is a good reason to ask another question.

Can I wait until the tooth hurts?

That can allow disease to progress. Ask whether monitoring is appropriate and what review interval or changes matter.

Does every black groove mean decay?

No. Staining and active lesions need clinical differentiation.

Will the filling prevent future cavities?

It repairs a particular defect; it does not remove decay risk elsewhere or at the margins.

Why might the plan change during treatment?

Access can reveal tissue conditions not fully visible beforehand. The reason, options and additional burden should be explained.

Take these questions to your consultation
  • Which findings show this lesion needs treatment now?
  • Can the pulp and more tooth structure be preserved?
  • What is driving my risk of another cavity?
